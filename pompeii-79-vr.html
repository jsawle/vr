A 3D reconstruction of Vesuvius before the AD 79 eruption, a broad flat-topped mountain without today's cone, with the walled town of Pompeii about 10 km to the south-east, Herculaneum on the coast to the west and Stabiae to the south. As the story plays, an eruption column rises from the mountain, pumice falls on the land to the south-east and covers Pompeii, and later ground-hugging currents of hot ash flow down the slopes. The panel describes each moment in words.

How this works

The volcano before the eruption. Today's cone (the Gran Cono, 1,281 m) grew after AD 79; its top was blown off in 1631 and rebuilt by eruptions up to 1944. Research finds no sign that a cone was built in the thousands of years before AD 79, though the shape of the summit before the eruption is still debated, and the geographer Strabo, writing before the eruption, described a mostly flat, barren, ash-coloured summit. So the model removes the cone and shows a broad, flat-topped Monte Somma with an old crater (caldera). The height of the caldera rim on the south side is not known; the model joins it up at about 990 m. It is a reconstruction, not a measurement (terrain model 0.1).

The town. The city wall, the ancient streets and the public buildings around the Forum, the Palestra Grande and the amphitheatre are placed from OpenStreetMap. Most houses are not mapped, so the houses are generated: plots along each block, one or two storeys, with gardens in the middle, as Roman houses had. Each house is an illustration, not the real house on that spot. The theatres and many other buildings are not in this version.

The people are simple figures walking the real streets. As the agreed approach for this case study, the app does not show anyone dying: people shelter or leave, and the view fades when the current arrives.

The eruption follows published research. Times are approximate and the date is debated (24 August in Pliny's letters, or October). The column reaches about 27 km, then about 33 km. The pumice fall is a simple model that matches the measured thickness: up to about 2.8 m at Pompeii and about 2 m at Stabiae, falling mainly to the south-east at a steady rate. Roofs fail at random between about 0.3 and 1 m of pumice, following studies of the ruins; which roof fails when is an illustration. The currents spread from the volcano at the times recorded; their speed in the app is not their real speed.

Not shown in this version: the AD 79 coastline (the sea then came about 1 km closer to Pompeii than it does today), the old course of the river Sarno, and the ground level of the plain, which was a few metres lower than today in places.
